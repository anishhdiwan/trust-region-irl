TRIRL Explained: Bridging Classical and Adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning

Anish Diwan [DATE — e.g. July 2026] ~8 min read
Introduction — placeholder A short hook on why teaching robots to imitate human behaviour is hard. Set up the tension between reward learning (understanding why the expert acts) and behavioural cloning (copying actions). Introduce IRL as the principled framing — then immediately raise the question: why isn't it the default?

1. What is Inverse Reinforcement Learning?

Explain IRL at a high level: given expert demonstrations, recover the underlying reward function. Use an intuitive analogy — e.g., learning the rules of chess from watching grandmaster games rather than memorising moves. Contrast with behavioural cloning and why IRL generalises better.

2. The Classical Approach — What Works and What Doesn't

Introduce Max Causal Entropy IRL (MCE-IRL). Explain the key guarantee: monotonic improvement of the imitation objective at every iteration. Then explain the critical limitation: to compute the reward gradient, you need the optimal policy for the current reward — which requires running RL to convergence. In high-dimensional environments, this is prohibitively expensive.
The core tension: Classical IRL has strong guarantees but requires solving a full RL problem each iteration — too expensive for real-world tasks.

3. The Modern Fix — Adversarial IRL

Explain how GAIL and variants (AIRL, AMP, LSIQ) reformulate MCE-IRL as a two-player min-max game. The discriminator provides local rewards; the policy takes a few gradient steps. This is cheap — but why is it so unstable? Trace the instability to two root causes: (1) the reward is local and changes every step; (2) the policy is never truly optimal for any reward.

4. TRIRL — The Key Idea

Present the main insight clearly. The question TRIRL asks: if we can only afford a local policy update (a trust-region step), what reward update is consistent with that? Answer: a smaller step in the same direction — on a corrected reward. Walk through the intuition: the trust-region policy is locally optimal for a big reward step, but globally optimal for a small corrected step. This means you get the MCE guarantee without running RL to convergence.
Run a cheap trust-region policy update → read off the Lagrange multiplier $\eta$ → shrink the reward step by $1/(1+\eta)$. The resulting pair is exactly MCE-valid.
Describe the discriminator buffer: because rewards are updated in function space, TRIRL maintains a small buffer of the $k$ most recent discriminators plus a fitted parametric reward for older history. This keeps memory bounded while preserving update accuracy.

5. Does it Actually Work?

Walk through the experimental results at a high level. Highlight the 2.4× IQM improvement. Pick two or three illustrative tasks and explain why TRIRL does better — e.g., on the G1 humanoid, where discriminator accuracy is critical. Connect back to theory: unlike adversarial methods, TRIRL benefits from a more accurate discriminator rather than being destabilised by it.

6. Global Rewards — a Bonus

Explain what "global reward" means and why it matters: a reward that can be re-optimized from scratch with a new random agent, and that transfers to environments with changed dynamics. Contrast with GAIL/AIRL where the reward is entangled with the training policy. Show an example: re-training TRIRL's reward on Ant with disabled legs still gives a meaningful locomotion reward.

7. Takeaways

Summarise the three key takeaways: (1) The gap between classical and adversarial IRL is not fundamental — it can be bridged with trust-region correction. (2) You don't need to sacrifice stability for efficiency. (3) Reward functions that are globally optimizable and transferable are achievable with the right formulation. End with a forward-looking note on extensions (f-divergences, more general dynamics, real-robot deployment).
